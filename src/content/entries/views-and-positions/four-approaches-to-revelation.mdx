Before any question about the millennium or the rapture can be answered, a prior question has to be settled: **what kind of thing is Revelation describing?** Is it first-century history in symbolic dress, a map of church history, a preview of the last few years before Christ returns, or a timeless picture of the church's struggle? The four classic answers are usually called preterist, historicist, futurist, and idealist. Almost every commentary is one of these, or a deliberate blend.

## At a glance

| | Preterist | Historicist | Futurist | Idealist |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Revelation 4–19 describes** | The fall of Jerusalem (AD 70) and/or of Rome | The whole sweep of church history | The final period before Christ's return | The conflict between Christ and evil in every age |
| **When fulfilled** | Mostly in the first century | Progressively, from John's day to the end | Still future | Continually |
| **"Soon" (1:1, 22:20)** | Taken at face value: soon for John's readers | Begins soon, unfolds for centuries | Refers to the whole complex of end events, or "quickly, once it starts" | Always near, because always true |
| **The beast** | Nero, or Rome | The papacy (classic Protestant reading) | A future Antichrist | Any state that demands worship |
| **Babylon** | Jerusalem or Rome | Papal Rome | A future world system | Every worldly city set against God |
| **Millennium** | Usually post- or amillennial | Usually postmillennial (historically) | Usually premillennial | Usually amillennial |
| **Notable holders** | Kenneth Gentry, R. C. Sproul, David Chilton, N. T. Wright (in part) | Luther, Calvin, Matthew Henry, Spurgeon, the Reformers generally | Walvoord, Ryrie, Mounce, Ladd, MacArthur | Hendriksen, Beale, Vern Poythress, Dennis Johnson |

## The approaches

### Preterist

From the Latin *praeter*, "past." Revelation was written to seven real churches facing real persecution, and it promised them that God would judge their persecutors *soon*. Partial preterists see the book fulfilled in the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 (chapters 6–19) and the fall of Rome, while chapters 20–22 remain future: Christ's return, the final judgment, and the new creation are still to come. Full preterists claim everything, including the second coming, happened in AD 70; that position denies a future bodily return and stands outside historic orthodoxy.

The preterist reading depends heavily on an early date for Revelation (before AD 70, under Nero). If the traditional date under Domitian (around AD 95) is right, the preterist case is much harder to make.

**Strength.** It takes the time markers seriously ("the time is near," 1:3) and reads the book as its first readers would have. **Weakness.** Much of the imagery (cosmic collapse, the whole world worshipping the beast) seems larger than a local Roman war, and the early date is a minority position among scholars.

### Historicist

Revelation is a continuous prophecy of church history. The seals, trumpets, and bowls are successive eras: the fall of Rome, the rise of Islam, the medieval papacy, the Reformation, and so on. This was the dominant Protestant reading from the Reformation through the nineteenth century, and it is why so many Reformers identified the papacy with the Antichrist.

**Strength.** It expects Revelation to matter to every generation of the church and takes the book's sense of movement seriously. **Weakness.** No two historicists agree on the timetable, and each generation has had to redraw it as history refused to end. The approach is almost unrepresented among current commentators, though it survives among Seventh-day Adventists.

### Futurist

From chapter 4 onward, Revelation describes events still to come: a seven-year tribulation, an Antichrist, the return of Christ, the millennium, and the judgment. The letters to the seven churches (chapters 2–3) are read either as first-century churches only or as a sketch of church history. Dispensational futurism reads the visions in strict chronological sequence and as literally as possible; moderate futurism (Ladd, Mounce) allows more symbolism and recapitulation but still places the fulfilment at the end of the age.

**Strength.** It preserves the plain sense of the book's climax: a real Antichrist, a real return, a real judgment. It also fits the way the New Testament elsewhere speaks of a future "man of lawlessness." **Weakness.** It has to explain why a book that says "the time is near" and was addressed to seven specific churches would be mostly irrelevant to them, and to every reader for two thousand years.

### Idealist

Revelation is not a coded timetable at all. It is apocalyptic, and apocalyptic works by symbol. The book shows, in vision after vision, the pattern that holds in every age: the church suffers, the world system rages, God judges, and the Lamb wins. The beast is Rome, and the empires after it, and whatever asks for our worship now. The visions recapitulate rather than progress: seals, trumpets, and bowls each cover the same ground from a different angle.

**Strength.** It respects the genre and explains why the book has spoken so directly to persecuted Christians in every century. **Weakness.** Pushed hard, it can dissolve the book's forward motion entirely, so that nothing in particular is ever predicted, even though John clearly expects a real end.

## The eclectic consensus

Most recent scholarly commentaries refuse to pick just one. Beale calls his view "eclectic" or "redemptive-historical idealist": the visions are primarily symbolic of the whole church age (idealist), but they had a real first-century referent (preterist) and they point to a real final climax (futurist). Mounce, a moderate futurist, grants the same. The practical result is that the two live options today are futurism and some idealist blend, and the argument between them is mostly about how much of chapters 6–19 is still to come.

<Callout variant="note" title="How this connects to the millennium">
  <p>Approach and millennial view are logically separate but tend to travel together. Futurists are usually premillennial, because they read Revelation 19 and 20 in sequence. Idealists and preterists are usually amillennial or postmillennial, because they read chapter 20 as recapitulating the church age. Deciding whether chapter 20 follows chapter 19 in time is the hinge that connects the two debates.</p>
</Callout>
