Sitting on the Mount of Olives opposite the temple, after telling his disciples that not one stone of it would be left on another, Jesus answers a question that his disciples had asked as one question and that turns out to be at least two.

<Scripture ref="Matthew 24:3">
  <p>"Tell us, when will these things be, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?"</p>
</Scripture>

Three things are bundled here: **when** the temple falls, the **sign** of Jesus' *parousia*, and the **end of the age**. Almost every disagreement about the discourse is a disagreement about which verses answer which part.

## The fork at verse 34

"Truly, I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things take place." Everything turns on the scope of *all these things*.

- **Preterist reading.** Verses 4–35 describe the run-up to and the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, within the lifetime of Jesus' hearers, exactly as he said. The "coming of the Son of Man on the clouds" in verse 30 echoes Daniel 7:13, where the Son of Man comes *to God* to receive a kingdom, not to earth; the cosmic language is standard prophetic imagery for the fall of a nation (Isaiah 13:10; 34:4). The real second coming begins at verse 36 ("but concerning that day"), marked by a change of subject and a change of tone: signs before, no signs after.
- **Futurist reading.** Verses 15–31 describe the final tribulation and the visible, bodily return. "This generation" means the generation alive when the signs begin, or "this race" (the Jewish people), or "this kind of (evil) generation." Verse 30 is the same event as 1 Thessalonians 4 and Revelation 19.
- **Both-and (the common evangelical view).** The fall of Jerusalem is a near fulfilment that prefigures the far one; the discourse deliberately telescopes the two, as Old Testament prophecy often does. Verses 4–28 have AD 70 primarily in view, verses 29–31 the end.

<Callout variant="note" title="Where the rapture views plant their flags">
  <p>Post-tribulation and pre-wrath readers point to verses 29–31: the Son of Man comes and gathers his elect with a trumpet <em>after</em> the tribulation, which they identify with 1 Thessalonians 4:16–17. Pre-tribulation readers reply that the discourse is addressed to Israel, not the church, and that the gathering in verse 31 is of Jewish believers into the kingdom, not the rapture. Whether Matthew 24 is "for the church" is therefore a load-bearing question.</p>
</Callout>

## Things to keep straight

1. Luke's version says nothing about an abomination; it says "Jerusalem surrounded by armies" (21:20). Whatever Matthew's phrase means, Luke understood it as an army at the gates.
2. The "one taken, one left" of 24:40–41 is often quoted for the rapture, but in the Noah comparison just before it, the ones *taken* are taken away by the flood in judgment. The verse may mean the opposite of what the bumper sticker says.
3. Mark's "not even the Son" (13:32) is the plainest statement in the Gospels of the limits of Jesus' knowledge in his humanity, and no timeline should claim more than he did.
