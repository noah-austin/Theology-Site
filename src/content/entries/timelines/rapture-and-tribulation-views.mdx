Every orthodox Christian view agrees on the ending: Christ returns bodily and visibly, the dead are raised, all are judged, and God dwells with his people forever. The disagreements are about the order of the events on the way there. Two questions do most of the work. First, **when is the church caught up to meet Christ** relative to the tribulation? Second, **what is the thousand years** of Revelation 20, and does Christ return before or after it?

The first chart takes the first question. The second chart takes the second. A table at the end shows how the answers pair up.

## Where the rapture falls

The four main rapture positions are all defined against the same framework: a future seven-year period, Daniel's seventieth "week," split in half by the abomination of desolation and ending at the Second Coming. The chart uses that framework as its axis. Views that reject the framework altogether (most amillennialists and postmillennialists) are functionally post-tribulational and are covered in the second chart.

### Pre-tribulation

**The claim.** The church is removed before the seventieth week begins, so it experiences none of the tribulation. The rapture and the Second Coming are two events separated by seven years. In between, the church is judged for rewards (the *bema*) and celebrates the marriage supper of the Lamb, then returns with Christ.

**Key texts.** 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 and 5:9 ("God has not destined us for wrath"); Revelation 3:10 ("I will keep you from the hour of trial"); John 14:1–3; 1 Corinthians 15:51–52; Titus 2:13. The argument from silence is also important: the church is not mentioned between Revelation 4 and 19.

**Held by.** John Nelson Darby, C. I. Scofield, Lewis Sperry Chafer, John Walvoord, Charles Ryrie, Dwight Pentecost, John MacArthur. The default position of dispensationalism and of most American evangelical popular teaching since the early twentieth century.

**Strongest point.** It takes imminence seriously: if Christ could come at any moment, nothing prophesied can stand between now and the rapture, and the seventieth week clearly must. It also keeps the promise of exemption from wrath at full strength.

**Main objection.** No passage plainly describes two future comings. Every text used for the rapture can also be read of the Second Coming, and the New Testament regularly tells the church to expect tribulation (John 16:33; Acts 14:22; 2 Thessalonians 1:4–7). Critics also note that the view is not attested before the 1830s.

### Mid-tribulation

**The claim.** The church is caught up at the midpoint of the seventieth week, after the first three and a half years but before the great tribulation. The "last trumpet" of 1 Corinthians 15:52 is identified with the seventh trumpet of Revelation 11:15.

**Key texts.** Daniel 9:27 and 7:25 (the three and a half years); Revelation 11:15–19; 1 Corinthians 15:52; 2 Thessalonians 2:1–4 (the man of lawlessness is revealed before "our being gathered to him").

**Held by.** Norman Harrison, Gleason Archer, J. Oliver Buswell, Merrill Tenney. Now a small minority; most of its adherents have moved to the pre-wrath view, which refines it.

**Strongest point.** It honours 2 Thessalonians 2, where Paul says the day of the Lord will not come until the apostasy and the revealing of the man of lawlessness, and it links the "last trumpet" to a trumpet that actually appears in Revelation.

**Main objection.** The seventh trumpet is a trumpet of judgment sounded by an angel, not the trumpet call of God in 1 Thessalonians 4:16, and nothing in Revelation 11 describes a rapture. The midpoint also seems to give the church no exemption from the first half of the week.

### Pre-wrath

**The claim.** The church goes through the great tribulation, which is the persecution of Antichrist, not the wrath of God. Jesus said those days would be "cut short" (Matthew 24:22). At that point, some time in the second half of the week, the church is raptured, and immediately afterward the day of the Lord's wrath (the trumpet and bowl judgments) falls on the earth. In Revelation the rapture sits between the sixth seal and the seventh.

**Key texts.** Matthew 24:29–31 (the coming "immediately after the tribulation of those days"); Revelation 6:12–17 and 7:9–14 (the great multitude "coming out of the great tribulation"); 2 Thessalonians 2:1–4; 1 Thessalonians 5:9; Joel 2:31.

**Held by.** Marvin Rosenthal, Robert Van Kampen, Alan Kurschner, Charles Cooper. First systematized in 1990; growing among people who find pre-trib exegetically thin but want to keep exemption from wrath.

**Strongest point.** It distinguishes what the text distinguishes: tribulation from wrath, Antichrist's persecution from God's judgment. It also reads Matthew 24, 2 Thessalonians 2, and Revelation 6–7 in a straightforward sequence.

**Main objection.** It requires the seals to be Antichrist's activity rather than the Lamb's judgments, though it is the Lamb who opens them. Critics also argue the trumpets and bowls are not clearly confined to a short window after the sixth seal.

### Post-tribulation

**The claim.** The church remains on earth through the whole tribulation and is caught up to meet Christ as he descends. The rapture and the Second Coming are one event: believers rise to meet the King in the air and escort him to earth, as a city went out to meet an arriving dignitary.

**Key texts.** Matthew 24:29–31; 2 Thessalonians 1:6–10 and 2:1–4; John 17:15 ("I do not ask that you take them out of the world, but that you keep them from the evil one"); Revelation 20:4–6 (the first resurrection follows the beast's reign); 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 read as the return itself.

**Held by.** George Eldon Ladd, Robert Gundry, Douglas Moo, Craig Blomberg, John Piper. The historic premillennial position, and, in effect, the position of the early church fathers who wrote about the Antichrist (Irenaeus, Hippolytus).

**Strongest point.** Simplicity. Every relevant passage speaks of one coming, and the church is told throughout the New Testament to expect suffering and persevere. The resurrection of believers is placed after the tribulation in Revelation 20.

**Main objection.** It struggles with imminence, since the tribulation must come first. It also has to explain who populates the millennium in mortal bodies if all believers are glorified at the return and all unbelievers are judged.

### Partial rapture

**The claim.** Only watchful, spiritually prepared believers are taken at the first rapture; the rest of the church remains through the tribulation to be purified, and is gathered at the end. Readiness, not simply faith, determines the timing.

**Key texts.** Luke 21:36 ("stay awake at all times, praying that you may have strength to escape all these things"); Matthew 25:1–13 (the wise and foolish virgins); Hebrews 9:28 ("to those who are eagerly waiting for him"); Philippians 3:11.

**Held by.** Robert Govett, G. H. Lang, G. H. Pember, Watchman Nee. Always a small minority.

**Main objection.** It divides the body of Christ and makes the rapture a reward for merit rather than the destiny of everyone who is "in Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:22–23; 1 Thessalonians 4:16, "the dead in Christ," without qualification).

## What the thousand years are

The second question is independent of the first, though the answers cluster. Revelation 20:1–6 describes Satan bound for a thousand years while the saints reign with Christ. The three families of interpretation take the "thousand years" as a future earthly reign after Christ returns (premillennialism), the present age viewed from heaven (amillennialism), or a coming era of gospel triumph before Christ returns (postmillennialism).

## Where the debate actually turns

Most arguments trace back to a few hinge questions. Deciding these settles most of the rest.

1. **Is Daniel's seventieth week future?** If the seventy weeks ran continuously and ended in the first century, the seven-year framework collapses and the rapture-timing debate mostly evaporates.
2. **Does "tribulation" mean God's wrath?** Pre-trib says yes, so the church must be gone. Pre-wrath and post-trib say no: tribulation is persecution, wrath is God's, and only the second is promised to be avoided.
3. **Is imminence "any moment" or "soon and unexpected"?** Only the strict reading demands a pre-trib rapture.
4. **Are Israel and the church one people?** If they are, the church's absence from Revelation 4–18 is not evidence of anything, since the saints there simply are the church.
5. **Is Revelation 20 chronologically after Revelation 19?** If yes, the return precedes the thousand years (premillennial). If chapter 20 recapitulates the church age, the return comes at the end (amillennial or postmillennial).
